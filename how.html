Knowledge Base

Practical privacy and security, from what privacy actually is, through a hardening manual, to the tools worth using and the fight to keep them legal.

Part I  ·  Foundations

Why privacy matters

Privacy is not about having something to hide. It is about deciding who gets to know what about you, and being able to change that decision later. Almost every practical step further down this page only makes sense once that idea is clear.

Four words that get mixed up

Most bad advice comes from treating these as the same thing. They are four separate properties, and you can have any one of them without the others.

They are independent. A public tweet from a burner account is anonymous but not secret. An encrypted message to a friend is secret but not anonymous, the app still knows the two of you talked. Security is the wall that makes the other three hold.

Privacy

Control. You decide what leaves you, who receives it, and for how long. Sharing a photo with five friends is privacy. Having it scraped into an ad profile is not.

Lost when: consent is assumed, not asked.

Secrecy

Concealment of content. End-to-end encryption gives you this: the carrier moves your message but cannot read it.

Lost when: someone else holds the key.

Anonymity

Separation of an action from your identity. Hard to achieve and easy to break, one reused username or login is enough.

Lost when: two identities share one detail.

Security

Resistance to attack. Patches, strong authentication, and sane defaults. Without it the other three are just promises.

Lost when: the weakest device wins.

“I have nothing to hide”

This is the most common objection, and it quietly swaps two different questions: are you doing something wrong? and does everyone have a right to watch you? Only the second one is about privacy.

If you've done nothing wrong, you have nothing to fear.
You already act otherwise. You close the bathroom door, you don't publish your salary, and you don't hand your unlocked phone to a stranger, not because of crime, but because context matters. Information that is harmless in one place is damaging in another: your address is fine with a courier and dangerous with a stalker.
I'm not interesting enough to be targeted.
Almost nobody is targeted personally. You are processed in bulk: scraped, scored, and sold alongside millions of others. Bulk collection does not check whether you are interesting first, and neither do the criminals who later buy the same database.
I'll worry about it if something happens.
Data is retroactive. What you leak today is stored and re-read years later, under future employers, future partners, future laws, and future governments. You cannot un-leak a database, and you cannot consent on behalf of the person you'll be in ten years.

The point of privacy is not to hide wrongdoing. It is to keep the ability to say “no”, and a right you only defend once you personally need it is not a right, it is a favour.

Foundational principle

Where your data actually goes

People imagine one company holding one profile. The reality is a market: dozens of collectors feed a handful of aggregators, who merge everything into a single identity graph and resell it. You are never asked, and you are rarely told.

Why the last box matters. The pipeline that sells you to advertisers is the same pipeline that sells you to whoever wants to find you. There is no separate, dirtier market for that. It is one market with different customers.

Decide what you're defending against

“Maximum privacy” is not a goal, it is a way to burn out. Every control costs convenience, so the useful question is narrower: who realistically wants your data, and what happens if they get it? Answer five questions and your list writes itself.

1 · What am I protecting?

Accounts, messages, location, identity documents, who you talk to. Be specific; “everything” is not an answer.

2 · Who wants it?

An ad network, a scammer, an ex-partner, a coworker, a government. Each has very different reach.

3 · What if they get it?

Annoying ads, drained bank account, doxxing, job loss, physical danger. This sets how much effort is rational.

4 · How likely is it?

Bulk scraping is near-certain. A targeted intrusion is rare for most people, and the defences differ completely.

5 · What will I actually keep doing?

A control you abandon in a week is worth zero. Pick the strongest thing you'll still use next year.

Adversaries are not interchangeable. The higher you go, the more expensive and targeted the capability becomes, and the fewer people it applies to.

01
Bulk collectors Trackers, brokers, scrapers. Not aimed at you; they take everyone. Beaten by blocking, aliases, and giving less away by default.
02
Opportunist criminals Credential stuffing, phishing, SIM swaps. They reuse leaked data at scale. Beaten by unique passwords and non-SMS 2FA.
03
Someone who knows you An ex, a rival, a coworker. Weak technically but rich in context, they already know your answers, habits, and devices.
04
Platforms & employers They hold the account and the logs. No hacking required: access is a policy decision, and policies change.
05
State-level actors Legal compulsion, purchased broker data, network position, device exploits. Rarely worth optimising for unless you have real reason to.
For almost everyone, tiers 1–3 are the whole game. Fixing those is cheap, permanent, and removes the overwhelming majority of real-world harm. Start there instead of chasing the last 1%.

How one small leak becomes everything

Breaches feel abstract until you follow the chain. A forgotten account from years ago is usually the first domino, and your inbox is almost always the last one, because it can reset everything else.

One habit does most of the work. Password reuse is what converts a stranger's breach into your problem. A password manager removes that link permanently, and everything after it stops working.

Not all leaks are the same size

People say "I was in a breach" as if it were one kind of event. There are really two, and the difference decides how much of your life is affected. A site breach exposes what you gave that one site. A stealer log exposes everything your computer had saved, because the malware copies the browser's own vault rather than attacking any website.

Why stolen cookies matter more than stolen passwords. A session cookie is proof you already logged in, so it walks past the password and the second factor entirely. That is why "I changed my password" often does not end a stealer-log compromise, and why signing out of all devices is a separate, necessary step.

Find out where you already appear

Guessing is the worst option. Before changing anything, look at what is actually out there under your name, because that tells you which accounts to fix first and which ones no longer matter.

What to do with what you find

A results page is only useful if it changes what you do next. Work top-down: the first two items remove most of the risk, and the rest is cleanup you can spread over a week.

01
Fix the inbox first, always If the leaked address is the one your other accounts reset through, it is the only account that matters this hour. New unique password, app or key based 2FA, then sign out every other session.
02
Kill the reuse, not just the leaked password The leaked password is already public. What actually hurts you is every other site where you used it, or a close variant of it. A password manager finds and replaces those in one pass.
03
If a stealer log shows up, assume the device That is not a password problem, it is an infected machine. Follow the incident steps further down this page, from a different device, before you reset anything.
04
Close what you no longer use Old accounts sitting in the results are pure liability. Delete them, or at minimum overwrite the details and disable the alias they were signed up with.
05
Then request removal Once the accounts are safe, start getting the records themselves taken down. This is slower and less urgent, but it is what shrinks the profile long term.
Appearing in a breach is normal. Most people with an email older than a few years are in several. It is not a sign you did something wrong, and it is not something to panic about. It is a list of which doors to change the locks on.
Part II  ·  How things work

Deep dives

The checklist in the next part tells you what to do. This part explains why it works, because a rule you understand is one you'll still follow in six months, and one you'll know when to break.

Signing up is permanent

Every account you create is a copy of your data on someone else's computer, governed by a document you didn't read and can change without asking you. You can close the account. You usually cannot un-give what you already handed over, and if that company is bought, breached, or goes bankrupt, your data is an asset that gets transferred.

So the useful habit is not "delete accounts later". It is give less at signup. Before creating one, three questions settle it: do I need an account at all, what is the least I can give, and can I recover this if I lose access?

What to look for in the terms nobody reads

You don't have to read 40 pages. Use your browser's find function on the privacy policy and search for a handful of words. The answers cluster in the same few places every time.

Two minutes, five searches. This finds more than reading the whole thing, because the parts that matter are legally required to be in there somewhere, just not in plain sight.

How you log in matters more than the password

Every service offers a few ways in, and they fail very differently. The tradeoff is always the same: convenience versus how many parties learn about your activity.

MethodWho learns about youMain failureVerdict
Email + password Just the service (use an alias and it learns less) Reuse and phishing Fine with a manager
Passkey Just the service Device loss if not synced Best available
“Sign in with Google/Apple/Discord” The service and the provider, who now logs where you go One account locks or loses everything Avoid
Magic link by email The service and your mail provider Inbox access = account access Only with a hardened inbox
Phone number The service, your carrier, and anyone who buys the number list SIM swap; permanent identifier Refuse when optional
Single sign-on is a tracking product wearing a convenience costume. It's genuinely fewer passwords, but you're telling one company every place you have an account, and handing them a switch that turns off all of them at once.

Closing the accounts you forgot

Old accounts are the ones that leak. They sit on software nobody patches, holding an email address and a password you used in 2017. You almost certainly have dozens you can't name.

Finding them is mechanical: search your inbox for welcome, verify your email, your receipt, and password reset. Then check your password manager, and your browser's saved logins. That covers most of them.

01
Overwrite before you delete Change the name, address, and birthday to junk, then delete. Many services keep a "deleted" record, so better it holds nonsense than your real details.
02
Check whether it actually deletes "Deactivate" often means hidden, not gone, and logging in once can restore everything. Look for the words permanent and cannot be undone.
03
Use the law where you have it In the EU/UK (GDPR) and parts of the US (CCPA and similar), a deletion request is binding and has a deadline. Email the privacy contact in the policy, one paragraph is enough.
04
Kill the alias, not just the account If you signed up with a per-service alias, disabling it cuts the service off permanently, even if they ignore your deletion request.

What makes a password strong

Not symbols. Length. Attackers don't sit there typing guesses, they run billions of attempts per second against a stolen database. What stops them is the sheer number of possibilities, and length grows that number far faster than special characters do.

The practical version: let a manager generate 20+ random characters for every account, and memorise exactly two passphrases, one for the manager, one for your device. Four or five random words, not a sentence you'd say out loud.

One more thing worth knowing: services store a hash, not your password, a one-way scramble. That's why a breach doesn't always leak passwords directly. But weak hashing (or a weak password) means the scramble gets reversed in hours, which is exactly how old breaches keep producing working logins years later.

Second factors, ranked honestly

Multi-factor authentication means proving something you know (password) plus something you have (a device) or something you are (fingerprint, face). All of them beat a password alone. They are not remotely equal.

Why passkeys win: a code is a secret you can be tricked into repeating. A passkey is a signature your device produces only for the exact site it was registered to, so "the user got fooled" stops being a possible outcome.

Turn SMS off where you can

If a service supports an app or a key, remove the phone number as a factor. Otherwise it stays as a bypass, and attackers will just use the weakest enabled method.

Save the backup codes

Every service shows one-time recovery codes when you enable 2FA. Put them in your password manager immediately. This is the step people skip and regret.

Have two, not one

Two security keys, or an authenticator with encrypted backup. A single device is a single point of failure, and losing it locks you out as thoroughly as any attacker could.

Watch the recovery path

Strong 2FA is pointless if "lost your key?" falls back to an email link or a support agent. Your account is only as strong as its weakest way back in.

Email was never built to be private

Email is from the 1970s, designed for a network where everyone was trusted. Privacy was added later, in layers, and none of those layers cover the whole journey. Understanding the route explains every limitation people find surprising.

The part that never gets fixed: even with perfect encryption of the body, the envelope is public by design; mail servers need it to route. Who you email and when is often more revealing than what you wrote.

TLS is not end-to-end

It protects the message between servers, like a courier's locked van. Each provider still opens the box on arrival. Useful, but not what most people picture.

PGP works and almost nobody uses it

Real end-to-end encryption, but both sides need keys and discipline, and it still exposes the envelope. If content must be private, use a messenger instead.

Treat email as identity, not chat

Its real job is proving you control an address so you can reset accounts. Guard it accordingly: strongest 2FA, no reused password, no shared access.

Aliases fix the linking problem

A different address per service means a breach can't be joined to your other accounts, and you learn exactly who leaked or sold you.

Tracking pixels: most marketing mail contains an invisible image that reports when you opened it, how often, and roughly where. Turning off automatic image loading kills this in one setting, and every major client has it.

Tor, explained without the mystique

A VPN moves your trust to one company. Tor removes the need to trust any single party by splitting the knowledge across three independent relays, none of which knows both who you are and what you're doing.

Three hops, on purpose. Linking you to a site would require the guard and the exit to be the same adversary, cooperating, at the same moment. Relays are run by thousands of unrelated volunteers, which is what makes that hard.

What it protects

Your IP from the site, and your browsing from your ISP or network. It also gets you around censorship; bridges disguise the connection where Tor itself is blocked.

What it doesn't

Anything you tell the site yourself. Log into your real account over Tor and you've just linked that account to a Tor session. The network can't undo that.

Don't customise the browser

Tor Browser makes everyone look identical. Resizing the window, adding extensions, or changing fonts makes you the one distinctive user, the exact opposite of the goal.

It's slow for a reason

Three volunteer relays, often on different continents. If something feels unusably slow, that's the design working, not a fault.

DNS: the log nobody thinks about

Before your browser can load anything, it asks a resolver to turn a name into an address. By default that question travels in plain text to your ISP, a timestamped list of every site you visit, which is trivially loggable and, in many countries, legally required to be kept.

Worth doing, easy to oversell. Encrypted DNS (DoH/DoT) removes one clear log and blocks malware domains for free, but it moves trust to whoever runs the resolver, and your destination is still visible. It's a hygiene step, not a cloak.

Your operating system sees everything

Every app you harden runs on top of the OS. If it reports on you, nothing above it can help, which is why this is the one layer where the choice of vendor genuinely matters.

Fix downward, not upward. A private browser on a telemetry-heavy OS is a lock on a door with no wall. This is also why the effort here is Tier 3: high impact, real cost.

Windows

Set up with a local account, not a Microsoft one. Then turn off advertising ID, activity history, "tailored experiences", and optional diagnostics. Use the Pro or Enterprise editions if you can, they expose more of these switches.

Telemetry can be reduced, not removed.

macOS

Better defaults than Windows, but tied to an Apple ID that syncs a lot by default. Turn on FileVault, review what iCloud is uploading, and disable analytics sharing.

Good privacy from third parties, less from Apple.

Linux

No telemetry business model behind it, encryption offered at install, and updates you control. Fedora and Debian are boring in the best way. This is the realistic escape hatch.

Boring is the feature.

Phones

Your phone knows more about you than your laptop ever will. GrapheneOS on a Pixel is the strongest option; a current iPhone is a reasonable second. An unsupported Android with no updates is the worst device you own.

Updates matter more than brand.
Updates beat everything else on this page. The overwhelming majority of real compromises use bugs that were fixed months earlier. An unpatched device undoes every other precaution you've taken.
Part III  ·  Practice

The field manual

Concrete steps, ordered roughly by how much they buy you. Open a section, do the items, move on. You do not need all of it at once. The first three sections cover most real risk.

FIELD MANUAL // OPERATIONAL PROTOCOLS

Identity & Privacy

EMAIL · USERNAMES · DIGITAL FOOTPRINT
Email Shielding
Do not publish your primary email address. Utilize aliases or disposable forwards for forums, giveaways, and secondary registrations.
Namespace Separation
Establish distinct usernames across separate platforms to deny cross-referencing capabilities to passive investigators.
Phone isolation
Avoid binding your physical phone number to services. Silent contact syncing algorithms will link your profiles together. Use burner or virtual numbers for validation tasks.
Domain Privacy
Enable WHOIS masking on registered domains. Public access returns owner name, contact emails, and physical location indexes.
Metadata Verification
Examine photos for context leaks. Reflected surfaces, location indicators, and matching image hashes allow correlation across platforms.
Data Brokers: Commercial aggregators collect and catalog personal metrics. Manual opt-out requests or automated tools like Incogni can significantly reduce your exposure index.
Check your exposure Request removal

Passwords & Authentication

MANAGERS · GENERATION · BEST PRACTICES
Cryptographic Managers
Deploy an audited password manager. Bitwarden provides open-source transparency; KeePass serves as an offline alternative.
Zero Password Reuse
A single breach enables credential stuffing attacks across other portals. Generate randomized, unique strings for every application.
Diceware Entropy
Establish master passphrases via physical dice and EFF word lists. Chains of 6+ words optimize human memory while maximizing defense against brute-forcing.
Avoid standard password hints or patterns. Ensure your offline master password paper recovery is stored in a physically secure container.
Bitwarden KeePass

Two-Factor Authentication

TOTP · HARDWARE KEYS · RECOVERY
Multi-Factor Mandate
Activate secondary authentication factors. SMS is prone to interception; utilize TOTP apps or physical security keys.
TOTP Applications
Deploy Aegis or Ente Auth. Backup structural configurations locally and encrypt database exports.
FIDO2 Hardening
Deploy hardware keys like YubiKey where possible. The cryptographic validation binding prevents authentication on spoofed domains.
SIM Swapping: Hackers spoof cellular authorization to clone your identity. Bind carrier lines with custom account PINs to prevent number migration.

Email Security

PHISHING · ALIASES · TRACKING PIXELS
Encrypted Hosting
Migrate critical vectors to privacy-focused servers such as ProtonMail.
Tracking Pixel Suppression
Configure client rules to block auto-rendering of images. Hidden tracking elements log IP addresses, opening timestamps, and browser configurations.
Masking Services
Generate unique email aliases for third-party configurations, preventing your primary address from leaking during corporate database breaches.

Messaging Apps

ENCRYPTION · TRUST · HARDENING
Trust Architectures
Mainstream chat architectures log metadata. Utilize SimpleX or Session to decouple chat streams from personal markers.
Self-Destruct Timers
Activate vanishing message parameters. Local data caches on foreign endpoints are a constant security exposure.
Media Hardening
Turn off automatic file downloads. Malicious payloads within media containers can initiate remote execution scripts.

Links, Files & Downloads

EXECUTABLES · SCANNING · VERIFICATION
Masked Extensions
Force operating system parameters to reveal all extensions. Malicious archives frequently double-format targets (e.g. report.pdf.exe).
Multi-Engine Verification
Scan downloaded scripts or binaries across various platforms like VirusTotal and Hybrid Analysis prior to execution.
Active Scripts
Avoid executing raw command lines or scripts copied from forums without verifying what each parameter does.

Browser Privacy

EXTENSIONS · COOKIES · SEARCH ENGINES
Privacy Engines
Deploy Brave or Firefox. Integrate uBlock Origin to scrub tracking codes.
Compartmentalization
Segregate profiles for personal identity vs anonymous navigation. Never merge credentials across these boundaries.
Tracking Protection
Block third-party tracking scripts. Configure settings to isolate cookies to their host domains.

VPN & Network Privacy

MULLVAD · DNS · WEBRTC LEAKS
Zero-Logs Encapsulation
Route connections through Mullvad. Use WireGuard protocols to minimize footprint.
WebRTC Leaks
Turn off WebRTC protocols in your browser settings. WebRTC leaks real local interfaces past active VPN boundaries.
Encrypted Domain Queries
Deploy encrypted DNS queries (DNS over HTTPS). Standard DNS lookups leak site addresses to your service provider in plaintext.

Browser Fingerprinting

CANVAS · WEBGL · BLENDING IN
Why adding privacy extensions can backfire. Every tweak makes you rarer, and rare is exactly what fingerprinting looks for. The winning move is not to be unusual, it is to be identical to thousands of other people, which is the one thing Tor Browser and Mullvad Browser are actually built to do.
Anonymity Pools
Do not randomize fingerprint fields manually. Your browser must blend in with thousands of other targets to avoid detection.
Isolation Tools
Deploy Mullvad Browser or Tor Browser. These standard configurations use strict canvas and window letterboxing protections by default.
Profile Testing
Validate your browser configuration parameters using tools like Cover Your Tracks. Aim for randomized or shared characteristics.

Device & System Security

ENCRYPTION · ANTIMALWARE · UPDATES
Cryptographic Volumes
Activate full-disk encryption. Deploy VeraCrypt or system-level encryption protocols to protect data from physical tampering.
Endpoint Safeguards
Configure baseline security updates. Run processes from restricted accounts instead of administrator profiles.
Physical Interface Locks
Do not connect unknown USB drives. Disable auto-run properties, cover physical cameras, and avoid using untrusted charging interfaces.

Data, Backups & Transfers

3-2-1-1 RULE · METADATA · CLOUD
EXIF Removal
Strip EXIF parameters from photos and files before sharing. EXIF tags contain GPS lookups, camera models, and timestamps.
3-2-1-1 Backup Plan
Store 3 copies of your data on 2 separate media formats, keep 1 off-site, and maintain 1 air-gapped copy fully disconnected from all network interfaces.
Client Encryption
Encrypt sensitive assets before uploading them to cloud interfaces. Storage providers should only ever store your ciphertext.

OPSEC & Mindset

PERSONAS · PATTERNS · AI EXPOSURE
Separation of Personas
Never link anonymous identities to your real name. Once linked, browser cookies or network histories can compromise all personas.
Writing Style & Timezones
Be mindful of typing patterns and consistent timezone activity. Stylometry engines can fingerprint writing styles and link accounts.
AI Input Shielding
Do not input sensitive code, documents, or keys into public AI portals. Your data is stored on remote servers for model updates.

Incident Response

COMPROMISED? DO THIS NOW
Assume it saw everything. Treat every password typed on that machine as public, every session as active, and every saved browser login as taken, until you have reset them from a device you trust.
Immediate Network Disconnection
Disconnect the network interface immediately. Remove ethernet cabling, and disable WiFi/Bluetooth hardware.
Session Isolation
Do not log into sensitive portals from a compromised device. Use a clean, isolated node to rotate credentials and revoke active sessions.
Zero-Trust Wipe
A compromised device cannot be trusted. If infected, wipe all partition tables completely and reinstall the operating system.

Ratted? What to Do?

RAT · STEALERS · FULL RECOVERY
Kill the Connection
Pull ethernet, disable Wi‑Fi and Bluetooth, and unplug the machine if you can. A live RAT can exfiltrate keystrokes, screenshots, and clipboard data in real time.
Do Not “Fix It” on the Same PC
Do not log into email, Discord, banks, or crypto wallets from the infected device. Assume every password you type is captured. Use a clean phone or another computer for recovery.
Revoke Sessions First
From a clean device, sign out of all sessions on Discord, Google, Microsoft, and any exchange or wallet. Rotate passwords starting with email. It is the recovery key for everything else.
Check What Was Stolen
Review saved browser passwords, autofill, crypto extensions, and 2FA backup codes stored locally. Token grabbers often harvest Discord tokens, cookies, and wallet seed phrases in one pass.
Assume Persistence
RATs hide in startup entries, scheduled tasks, services, and AppData folders. Deleting one file rarely removes the infection. Treat the whole install as compromised.
Wipe and Reinstall
Back up only personal files you can verify (photos, documents, not executables). Format the drive, reinstall Windows from official media, and patch before restoring data.
After recovery: Enable hardware or app-based 2FA everywhere, use a password manager on the clean system, and never run cracked games, “free Nitro” tools, or unknown .exe files again. That is how most RATs land.
Warning signs: Mouse moving alone, webcam LED flicker, unknown processes in Task Manager, new startup programs, friends getting scam DMs from your account, or wallets draining without your action.

Common Cyber Attacks

KNOW WHAT YOU'RE UP AGAINST

Ransomware

Encrypts your local files and demands payment to restore access keys.

Phishing

Socially engineered targets designed to capture credentials via spoofed portals.

DDoS

Flooding network targets with traffic to degrade infrastructure performance.

MITM

Intercepting communication streams between target interfaces to extract data.

SQL Injection

Injecting backend queries into active variables to extract server records.

Zero-Day

Targeting unpatched security bugs before updates become available.

Part IV  ·  Tooling

Recommendations

Tools that hold up when you actually read how they work: open where it counts, audited, and not funded by selling you. Marked PICK = the one to take if you don't want to compare. Everything here is free unless noted.

How to read this list. No tool makes you private on its own, and no list stays correct forever. Ownership changes, audits expire, and defaults get worse. Treat these as starting points and re-check anything you rely on once a year.
Diminishing returns are real. Tier 1 removes most of the harm most people will ever face. Tier 3 exists for journalists, activists, and anyone with a named adversary, it costs real convenience, so adopt it deliberately, not for the aesthetic.

Part V  ·  Beyond yourself

Privacy is not a solo project

You can harden every device you own and still lose, because the rules are set somewhere else. Individual defence buys you time; it does not change the system. This part is about the other half.

Why it stops being a personal choice

Privacy behaves like a collective good, not a personal preference. If you use an encrypted messenger and everyone you know uses an open one, your conversations are still collected, from the other side. Your contacts upload your number. Your friends tag your face. Your landlord digitises your documents. Most of your exposure is generated by other people's defaults.

It also has a chilling effect that never shows up in statistics. People search less, read less, and say less when they assume they're being logged, and the ones who self-censor first are usually the ones with the least power to begin with.

Surveillance is rarely built for the population it ends up being used on. Systems built for emergencies get inherited by whoever governs next.

The ratchet problem

The backdoor argument, drawn out

The recurring proposal, under names like client-side scanning, lawful access, or "responsible encryption", is to give authorities a way to read encrypted messages. The technical objection is not ideological. A door that opens for one party is a door.

There is no "good guys only" cryptography. Access is granted by holding a key, not by having good intentions, so every backdoor is also a permanent, high-value target. The same reasoning applies to scanning your messages on-device before they're encrypted: once the scanner exists, what it looks for is a policy setting.

What you can actually do

Ordered by effort. The first two cost nothing and matter more than people expect, because they change the defaults around you rather than only your own.

01
Bring one person with you Encryption is worth more the more people use it. Move one conversation to Signal and you've just protected two people, and normalised it for a third.
02
Stop treating "nothing to hide" as harmless It's the sentence every expansion of surveillance is built on. Answering it well, once, in a normal conversation does more than a hundred hardened configs.
03
Exercise your legal rights Request your data, then demand deletion. Every request costs the company money and creates a record. Rights that go unused get quietly written out.
04
Fund the infrastructure A few euros a month to any group above buys more privacy than the next tool you'd install. They're funded by individuals precisely so they can't be captured.
05
Watch the legislation where you live Scanning mandates and age-verification schemes get reintroduced under new names until one passes. Consultations are usually public, and comments are counted.
06
Contribute capacity, not just money Run a Tor relay, translate documentation, file bug reports, or set up a friend's password manager. Most of this work is unglamorous and chronically short-handed.

You do not have to become an activist. You do have to stop assuming someone else is holding the line, because the people who are, are volunteers, and there aren't many of them.
